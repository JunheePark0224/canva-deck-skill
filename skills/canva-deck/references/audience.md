# Audience — slides people can read from the back of the room

A deck is read at a distance, in a few seconds, while someone is talking. Each rule below came from fixing a real deck after
it was "done". Run through this list in stage 3 for every slide, before hosting.

## 1. One place to look

- **One focal point per slide.** Decide what the eye should land on first: the title, one number or one row. Everything else
  is quieter (smaller, `--ink-soft`/`--ink-muted`). If two numbers compete, the audience reads the wrong one while you talk about
  the other. In the real deck, side counts ("16 people", "8 people") pulled the eye away from the point. They were cut, and the
  slide said one thing.
- **Accent marks the point, grey marks the rest.** Highlight the thing you are talking about with the accent or a dark fill,
  and leave the alternatives grey. Make highlighted items look the same as each other (if ⓑ and ⓒ are both "chosen", both get the
  same white text on dark). A half-highlighted item reads as a third category.
- **Every element earns its place.** For each small line, ask "would anyone miss it if it were gone?" Footnotes that repeat
  something said elsewhere ("data period: …" on a slide that is not about data) go to the slide where they belong, or go.

## 2. Size and contrast

At 1920×1080 (sizes in px; projected, 1px ≈ 0.75pt):

| Text | Size | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Slide title | 64–80 | the conclusion sentence, title font, bold |
| Key number | 120–300 | one per slide |
| Body / card text | 26–32 | never below 24 |
| Labels, captions, page numbers | 22–26 | `--ink-muted`; nothing smaller than 22 |

- If the text doesn't fit at these sizes, there is too much text. Cut words or split the slide. Don't shrink the font.
- Text on any background needs a contrast of at least 4.5:1. White or light text on a mid-tone photo or colour disappears
  (a white title on a busy image was unreadable). Put light text only on the dark cloth colour or the ink colour.
- Body lines stay under about 40 Korean characters (about 60 Latin characters). Longer lines lose the reader between lines.

## 3. Words

- **Titles are conclusions** (SKILL.md stage 1), and they are precise. One wrong word changes the claim. "They left without
  picking" is a different claim from "They saw the bookmarks and still left without picking".
- **No chains of fragments.** Strings like "A · B · C — D" read as code. Judges asked for clean sentences instead. Use a
  sentence, or a list with one idea per line.
- **Cut vague sentences.** If you can't say in one breath what a sentence means, the audience can't either. Delete it, or
  replace it with the concrete fact behind it.
- **Labels say what the slide is about.** The small label above a title ("DECISION") should name this slide's role in the
  story ("WHY A/B — HYPOTHESIS 2"), so someone who looked away knows where they are.

## 4. Order

- Follow the order the audience needs, not the order you worked in: problem → how we measure it → cause → idea → test →
  result. Put the problem before the metric, so the metric answers a question the audience already has.
- Close by returning to the opening line, then say thank you on the last slide. If the last slide also carries content (links,
  contact), make that content smaller so "thank you" can sit on the same slide.

## 5. The check (stage 3, every slide)

Look at each screenshot at about 1/4 size, which is roughly how it looks from the back row:
1. Where does the eye land first? Is that the point of the slide?
2. Can every word be read? Is anything under 22px, or light-on-light?
3. Is there one accent, and does it mark the point?
4. Is there a line nobody would miss? Cut it.
5. Read only the titles of the whole deck in order. Do they tell the story?
